## Level 1: Junior

### Scene 8 — On camera

> Level 1 is the junior data engineer, usually with zero to two years of experience. At this level, you build what someone else has designed. A senior engineer plans the pipeline, and you write the code. A pipeline is a set of steps that moves data from where it is created to where people use it, such as moving every sale on a shop's website into a daily sales report.

### Scene 9 — Graphic 4, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` Three task cards: "Write SQL to clean raw orders", "Fix a job that failed overnight", "Add a test: every order has a price". `[pause]`

> A normal week for a junior data engineer can include three kinds of tasks. You write SQL, the language used to ask a database questions, to turn raw orders into a clean table. You fix a job that failed overnight. You also add tests, such as a check that every order has a price.

### Scene 10 — Card beside you
**Do:** Card titled "What I test at Level 1": SQL and joins, basic Python, one project you can explain.

> When I interview a junior data engineer, I check three things. I test SQL, including joins, which combine two tables using a column they share, such as linking orders to customers by the customer ID. I check basic Python. Then I ask you to explain one project you built yourself, line by line, because a copied project falls apart after two or three questions.

### Scene 11 — Graphic 5, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` Two bars: "Typical US company: $126K" and "Meta: $168K". Small text under them: "Median total pay, Glassdoor (Data Engineer I). Average total pay, Levels.fyi (Meta IC3)." `[pause]`

> A junior data engineer at a typical company in the United States earns a median of about one hundred twenty-six thousand dollars a year in total pay. The median is the middle number, so half of the people earn more and half earn less. At Meta, the entry level for data engineers pays about one hundred sixty-eight thousand dollars.

### Scene 12 — On camera
**Do:** Bridge to the next part.

> To move from Level 1 to Level 2, take full ownership of one pipeline. When that pipeline breaks, you are the person who fixes it, and you are the person who explains why it broke and how you stopped it from happening again.

## Level 2: Mid-level

### Scene 13 — On camera

> Level 2 is the mid-level data engineer, usually with two to five years of experience. You now get a problem, not a plan. You decide how to solve the problem, you build the whole pipeline, and you own it after it goes live.

### Scene 14 — Graphic 6, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` A flow from left to right: "Marketing needs yesterday's customer data by 8 a.m." then "Choose the source", "Build the steps", "Add checks that catch wrong values", "Alert when the job fails". `[pause]`

> A typical Level 2 task sounds like this. The marketing team needs yesterday's customer data every morning by eight o'clock. You choose where the data comes from, build the steps, add checks that catch wrong values, and set an alert that tells you when the job fails.

### Scene 15 — Card beside you
**Do:** Card titled "What I test at Level 2": design a pipeline, late data, running the job twice.

> When I interview a mid-level data engineer, I give you a business request and ask you to design the pipeline. Then I ask what happens when data arrives late, and what happens when the job runs twice. A good answer explains how the pipeline gives the same result on a rerun, so a second run does not count every sale two times. Engineers call this property idempotency.

### Scene 16 — Graphic 7, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` Two bars: "Typical US company: $141K" and "Meta: $226K". Small text: "Glassdoor (Data Engineer II). Levels.fyi (Meta IC4)." `[pause]`

> A mid-level data engineer at a typical company earns a median of about one hundred forty-one thousand dollars a year. At Meta, the same level pays about two hundred twenty-six thousand dollars.

### Scene 17 — On camera
**Do:** Bridge to the next part.

> To move from Level 2 to Level 3, learn to explain trade-offs. A trade-off is what you give up when you pick one option over another. For example, batch processing moves data on a schedule, such as once a night, and usually costs less. Streaming moves each record the moment it arrives, such as a bank checking each card payment for fraud, and usually costs more. A senior engineer can say which one fits and why.

## Level 3: Senior

### Scene 18 — On camera

> Level 3 is the senior data engineer, usually with five to eight years of experience. A senior engineer designs systems for a whole team. You choose the tools, review the team's code, and help the juniors improve.

### Scene 19 — Graphic 8, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` A simple warehouse diagram: raw tables, then clean tables, then report tables, with a monthly cost label beside it. `[pause]`

> A typical senior task is deciding how the team's data warehouse is laid out. A data warehouse is a database built for reports and analysis, such as Snowflake or BigQuery. The senior engineer decides which tables exist, how they link, and how much the whole setup costs to run every month.

### Scene 20 — Card beside you
**Do:** Card titled "What I test at Level 3": system design, trade-offs, cost, failure, leading people.

> When I interview a senior data engineer, the main round is system design. I ask you to design the data platform for a product, such as a food delivery app, and I listen for trade-offs, cost, and what happens when something fails. I also ask about a time you led a project or helped another engineer improve.

### Scene 21 — Graphic 9, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` Two bars: "Typical US company: $177K" and "Meta: $311K". Small text: "Glassdoor (Senior Data Engineer). Levels.fyi (Meta IC5)." `[pause]`

> A senior data engineer at a typical company earns a median of about one hundred seventy-seven thousand dollars a year. At Meta, the same level pays about three hundred eleven thousand dollars.

### Scene 22 — On camera
**Do:** Bridge to the next part.

> To move from Level 3 to Level 4, solve a problem that more than one team has. Promotion to staff depends on the size of your impact, not on your years. At many companies, people stay senior for their whole career, and that is still a strong career.

---

## Level 4: Staff

### Scene 23 — On camera

> Level 4 is the staff data engineer, usually with eight or more years of experience, and some companies add a principal level above it. A staff engineer sets the direction for many teams and writes less code than a senior does. Most of the work is design documents, reviews, and decisions that other teams follow.

### Scene 24 — Graphic 10, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` One team that produces data on the left, an agreement in the middle listing columns and their meaning, and several teams that use the data on the right. `[pause]`

> A typical staff task is setting one standard for the whole company, such as data contracts. A data contract is an agreement between the team that produces a table and the teams that use it. The agreement says which columns will arrive and what they mean, so a change in one app does not break ten reports.

### Scene 25 — Card beside you
**Do:** Card titled "What I test at Level 4": a decision that changed how several teams work, what it cost, how you got agreement.

> When I interview a staff data engineer, I ask about decisions, not tools. I ask you to describe a decision that changed how several teams work, what it cost, and how you got people to agree with it.

### Scene 26 — Graphic 11, full screen
**Do:** `[pause]` Two bars: "Typical US company: $195K" and "Meta: $449K". Small text: "Glassdoor (Staff Data Engineer). Levels.fyi (Meta IC6)." `[pause]`

> A staff data engineer at a typical company earns a median of about one hundred ninety-five thousand dollars a year. At Meta, the same level pays about four hundred forty-nine thousand dollars.

### Scene 27 — Graphic 12, full screen

At Meta, each promotion is bigger than the one before, and the largest one, about one hundred thirty-eight thousand dollars, comes when you move from senior to staff.
