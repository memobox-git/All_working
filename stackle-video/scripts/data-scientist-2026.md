## Script

This script uses short, plain sentences and one everyday example for each idea. Every sentence is a full sentence, so it stays easy to follow for people who speak English as a second language.

Labels: **Graphic N, full screen** means the graphic fills the screen while you keep talking. **Card beside you** means you stay on screen with a card next to you. **On camera** means only you. *(pause)* means a one-second pause, where a graphic starts or ends.

### Hook

**[Graphic 1, full screen: the pay number and the job growth number]** *(pause)*

The middle of the pay range for a data scientist in the United States is about one hundred twenty thousand dollars a year. The government expects data scientist jobs to grow by about thirty-five percent by 2035, which makes it one of the ten fastest-growing jobs in the country. *(pause)*

**[On camera]**

So why do so many people try to become a data scientist and get stuck? Job posts for data roles are still far below their 2022 peak, so you need a clear plan. Most people learn things in the wrong order, and they build the same projects as everyone else.

### Promise

**[On camera]**

In this video I am going to show you how to become a data scientist in 2026 and 2027, step by step. You will learn if you need a degree, what to learn and in what order, how AI changed the job, the easier first job that most people skip, the projects that get callbacks, and how to pass the interviews.

### Intro

**[Intro graphic, full screen: the existing Stackle intro]** *(pause)*

If you are new here, I am Chris. I have worked in data engineering for more than ten years. I have interviewed more than two hundred engineers for companies in healthcare, banking, automotive, and financial exchanges. Today I run Stackle, where I teach data and AI engineering, and more than two hundred thousand people follow me on LinkedIn. *(pause)*

### Part 1: Do you need a degree?

**[On camera]**

Do you need a master's degree or a PhD to become a data scientist? The honest answer is no, but a degree helps more here than in most data jobs.

**[Graphic 2, full screen: six job posts, four asking for a master's or PhD]** *(pause)*

A 2025 study looked at data scientist job posts in the United States. About two in three mentioned a master's degree or a PhD. Almost one in five did not mention education at all. *(pause)*

**[Card beside you: "What I check"]** *(pause)*

A job post can ask for a degree, but the interview tests skills. When I interview people for data roles, I check three things. Can you write clean Python and SQL? Can you build a model and tell me why you chose it? Can you explain the result to a manager who does not code? *(pause)*

**[On camera]**

A degree in math, statistics, computer science or economics is a head start, not a finish line. Statistics takes the longest to learn on your own, so people with that background move faster. If you do not have a master's degree, the side door I show you later in this video is your best way in.

### Part 2: The skills, in order

**[Graphic 3, full screen: the four phases]** *(pause)*

Data science skills build on each other, like cooking. You learn to use a knife before you cook a full meal, so the order matters. There are four phases: Python and SQL, statistics, machine learning, and AI.

**[Graphic 4, full screen: Python and SQL]**

Phase one is Python and SQL. Python is the main language of data science. Learn the basics first, then pandas, a library that cleans and reshapes tables. Then learn Matplotlib or Seaborn, which turn numbers into charts.

SQL, which stands for Structured Query Language, is how you ask a database a question. If Python is your kitchen, SQL is the grocery store, because it is where you get the ingredients. Learn joins, which connect two tables, and aggregations, which add things up, like total sales for each month. Then learn window functions, which compare a row with the rows next to it, and subqueries, which put one question inside another. Even if the job is all about models, expect a SQL test in the interview.

**[Graphic 5, full screen: statistics]**

Phase two is statistics, which tells you if a result is real or just luck. If you flip a coin ten times and get seven heads, is the coin broken? That is exactly the question it answers.

Learn distributions, which describe the shape your data makes. Learn hypothesis testing, which is a fair way to check a claim. A p-value tells you how surprising a result would be if nothing had changed. A confidence interval gives you a range where the true number probably sits. Then practice A/B testing. Show half your users a green button and half a blue button, and measure which one gets more clicks. Interviewers love statistics questions, because the answers show how you think.

**[Graphic 6, full screen: machine learning, then back to the four phases with AI circled]**

Phase three is machine learning, which means teaching a computer to find patterns in past data so it can guess about new data. Your email spam filter is machine learning. It learned from millions of emails that people marked as spam.

Start with supervised learning, where every example comes with the right answer. Linear regression predicts a number, like the price of a house. Logistic regression predicts yes or no, like whether a customer will cancel. Then learn decision trees, which ask a chain of yes-or-no questions, and random forests and gradient boosting, which combine many trees into one stronger model. After that, learn unsupervised learning, where there is no answer key. K-means puts similar customers into groups, and PCA shrinks many columns into a few. The main library for all of this is scikit-learn.

Then learn how to grade a model. Accuracy alone can fool you. Think of a test for a rare disease where only one person in a hundred is sick. A model that says nobody is sick is ninety-nine percent accurate, and it is useless. That is why you learn precision, which tells you how many of your yes answers were right, and recall, which tells you how many of the real yes cases you found. AUC is a single score for how well your predictions separate yes from no. Later, take a first look at deep learning, the kind of machine learning behind image and speech tools, with PyTorch or TensorFlow. It is not needed for most first jobs. *(pause)*

### Part 3: How AI changed the work

**[On camera]**

Phase four is AI, and AI changed this job in two big ways.

The first change is how you work. Most data scientists now write code with a coding assistant, such as GitHub Copilot, Cursor or Claude Code. When your code breaks, you paste the error into a large language model, or LLM, the kind of model behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, and ask what went wrong.

**[Graphic 7, full screen: how you work, 83%]** *(pause)*

In the 2026 Stack Overflow survey, eighty-three percent of developers said they use at least one AI tool. But treat an assistant like a smart new intern. The intern works fast, and you still check every piece of work before it goes to your boss. So statistics and SQL matter more now, not less. They are how you check the work. *(pause)*

**[On camera]**

The second change is what you work on. Data scientists now work with a lot more text, like reviews, emails and support tickets. So you need to know when to use an LLM and when to use a classic model.

**[Graphic 8, full screen: classic model or LLM]** *(pause)*

Use a classic model when your data is a table of numbers. If you want to predict which customers will cancel, a model like gradient boosting is faster, cheaper and easier to check. Use an LLM when your data is messy text. If you want to sort ten thousand support emails by topic, an LLM can do it in minutes. LLMs work with embeddings, which are lists of numbers that capture what text means, and vector databases, which find the passages closest in meaning to a question. *(pause)*

**[On camera]**

Add one AI project to your portfolio. For example, build a tool that answers questions from a set of documents, or test how often an LLM gets the right answer on your own list of questions. A project like that shows a hiring manager that you know how the job looks in 2026 and 2027.

Here is the skill most people forget: explaining. A model is only worth something when a manager acts on it. Practice on a friend who does not work in tech. If your friend understands what your model does in one minute, you are ready.

### Part 4: The side door

**[On camera]**

Data science is often not the first job. Your first job in data may have a different title, and that is completely fine.

**[Graphic 9, full screen: the side door]** *(pause)*

Three jobs are more open to beginners: data analyst, product analyst, who studies how people use an app, and analytics engineer, who builds clean tables for the rest of the team. Those jobs already use SQL, statistics and charts every day, which is half the work of a data scientist. Think of this path as the side door into a building when the front door is locked. After a year or two, you move across inside the same company. A move inside a company is much easier than a new hire from outside, because the team already trusts your work. *(pause)*

**[Card beside you: "Your past industry counts"]** *(pause)*

Your past industry also counts. If you worked in healthcare, banking or retail, apply to data teams in that industry first. A person who already knows what a patient record or a credit score is needs less training. Hiring managers notice that. *(pause)*

### Part 5: Projects that get callbacks

**[Graphic 10, full screen: Titanic and Iris crossed out]** *(pause)*

Projects copied from a tutorial get skipped. The Titanic survival model and the Iris flower model are the two most famous beginner datasets, and hiring managers have seen them many times. Think of a cover song. It shows that you can follow along, but it does not show what you can make.

**[Graphic 11, full screen: the four questions]**

Every project should answer four questions. Who has the problem? What does the problem cost them? What did the data show? What should they do now? The last question matters most, because a manager pays for decisions, not for accuracy scores.

**[Graphic 12, full screen: build in a field you know, then the whole board]**

Build in a field you already know. If you worked in retail, predict which products will sell out. In a hospital, predict which patients will miss appointments. In sales, find which leads will turn into customers. *(pause)*

**[Card beside you: "3 projects"]** *(pause)*

Three real projects beat ten copied ones. Each project should go from messy data to a clear answer. Write the first page, the README, so that someone outside tech understands it in one minute. Then share each one on LinkedIn, because work that nobody sees cannot get you a callback. *(pause)*

### Part 6: The interviews

**[Graphic 13, full screen: the five stages]** *(pause)*

A data science interview usually has five stages. The first stage is a call with a recruiter. The second is a screen on SQL and statistics. Then come the final rounds: a machine learning case, a product question, like how you would test a new feature, and questions about how you work with people. Some companies also give you a take-home project. *(pause)*

**[On camera]**

Treat SQL practice like the gym. Ten minutes every day beats five hours the night before an interview. Sites like DataLemur, StrataScratch and LeetCode give you questions to practice on.

**[Card beside you: "Explain the why"]** *(pause)*

In the machine learning case, the why matters more than the what. A weak answer is, "I used logistic regression." A strong answer is, "I used logistic regression, because the manager needed to see which things push customers to cancel, and this model shows that clearly." The first one tells me what you did. The second one tells me how you think. *(pause)*

**[Graphic 14, full screen: the STAR method]** *(pause)*

For questions about past work, use the STAR method: the situation, the task, the action you took, and the result. Always end with a number. "My model found the customers most likely to cancel, and the team kept two hundred of them in one quarter" beats "my model worked well" every single time. *(pause)*

**[On camera]**

Before you send an application, look at who you already know. Search the company on LinkedIn and look for friends, old classmates, or friends of friends who work there.

**[Graphic 15, full screen: referrals, then the whole board]** *(pause)*

A referral means a person inside a company recommends you. Hiring data from Ashby, which makes hiring software, shows that referrals are about one percent of applications but about seventeen percent of hires. *(pause)*

### Your turn

**[On camera]**

Here is your homework for this week. Pick one public dataset from a field you know. Before you open Python, write the answers to the four questions. That one page will guide your whole project.

### Call to action

**[On camera]**

If you want help with your plan, book a free fifteen-minute career call with me. The link is in the description. If you want to go further into the AI side of this work, the Stackle AI Engineering Masterclass shows you how to build with large language models.

Tell me in the comments which phase you are in right now.

**[End screen: the existing Stackle CTA graphic, 11 seconds]**
