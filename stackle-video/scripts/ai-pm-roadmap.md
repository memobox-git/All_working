## Part 1: What an AI product manager does

**[Graphic 2, full screen]** Two columns. Left: "Decide what to build and why." Right: "Know how the AI behaves, what it costs, and how to test it."

*[pause]*

A product manager decides what a team builds and why. The product manager talks to users, picks the most important problem, writes down what success looks like, and makes sure the team ships it.

An AI product manager does all of that, plus one extra job. Normal software gives the same result every time you ask the same question. A model can give a different answer each time, and some of those answers can be wrong. Someone has to decide whether the model is good enough to put in front of real users, and that decision belongs to the AI product manager.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 3, full screen]** A bank chatbot. The same question is asked in two different ways. The first reply is correct. The second reply invents a charge, and it is marked in red.

Here is an example. A bank adds a chatbot that answers questions about charges. On Monday, a customer asks about late fees and gets the right answer. On Tuesday, another customer asks the same thing in different words, and the bot invents a fee that does not exist. Catching that mistake before real people see it is the AI product manager's job.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]**

The job therefore has two halves. First, you decide what to build. Second, you understand how the AI behaves, what it costs, and how to test it. Product managers usually arrive with the first skill set, and engineers usually arrive with the second. The people who get hired bring both, and this roadmap teaches both.

## Part 2: The salary

**[On camera]**

A good roadmap also tells you what the job pays. The numbers in this part come from real job ads posted in the United States between August and October 2026. Each number is a base salary, which means the pay before any bonus or stock.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 4, full screen]** A table with three rows. Entry: about $120,000 to $185,000, and closer to $90,000 in smaller markets. Senior: about $130,000 to $210,000. Lead and principal: $140,000 to $304,000. A note under the table: "All US product managers, total pay: median about $230,000 (Levels.fyi)."

At the entry level, which means associate and new graduate jobs, posted base pay runs from about one hundred twenty thousand to one hundred eighty-five thousand dollars. A new graduate AI product manager job at a large social media company lists one hundred twenty-two thousand to one hundred eighty-four thousand dollars. In smaller markets and outside the tech industry, starting pay can be closer to ninety thousand dollars.

At the senior level, posted base pay runs from about one hundred thirty thousand to two hundred ten thousand dollars. A senior job that focuses on AI agents and testing pays one hundred sixty-five thousand to two hundred thousand dollars, and that job is fully remote.

At the lead and principal level, the range gets wide. The lowest posting I found lists one hundred forty thousand dollars. The highest lists up to three hundred four thousand dollars, for a principal job building vision AI, which is AI that understands images and video, at a restaurant software company.

Base salary is not the whole package. Total pay adds bonus and stock on top of it. Levels.fyi, a site where tech workers share what they earn, reports a median total pay of about two hundred thirty thousand dollars a year for product managers in the United States, across all types of product jobs.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 5, full screen]** The bottom row of the table says "Help ship a feature." The top row says "Decide if an AI system is safe, useful, and worth its cost." An arrow runs from the bottom row to the top row.

Look at what changes from the bottom row to the top row. An entry job asks you to help ship a feature. A principal job asks you to decide whether an AI system is safe, useful, and worth its cost. You are paid for the size of the decision a company trusts you to make.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]**

All of these numbers come from the United States. Pay in other countries is usually lower, but the levels and the skills stay the same.

## Part 3: The seven skills

**[On camera]**

The skills are what move you from the bottom row to the top row. I read the requirements in the job ads for this video, and the same skills appear again and again. I grouped them into seven.

**[Card beside you]** 1. How LLMs work. 2. RAG, agents and fine-tuning. 3. Evals. 4. Quality, speed and cost. 5. Data and SQL. 6. Prototypes. 7. Specs and guardrails.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 6, full screen]** A phone keyboard that suggests the next word, beside three labels: "Token: about three quarters of a word." "Context window: how much text the model can read at one time." "Hallucination: a false statement said with confidence."

Skill one is knowing how large language models work. A large language model, or LLM, is the type of AI behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. An LLM predicts the next word, much like your phone suggests what comes next when you type a message. You also need three related terms. A token is a small piece of text, about three quarters of a word in English, and every token costs money. The context window is the amount of text the model can read at one time. A hallucination is a false statement said with full confidence, like the fee that the bank bot invented.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 7, full screen]** Three small diagrams side by side. RAG: search documents, then answer. Agent: the LLM takes an action. Fine-tuning: an existing model is trained further on your own examples.

Skill two is knowing the main ways teams build with LLMs. Retrieval augmented generation, or RAG, means the system first searches your company's documents and then hands the right pages to the model before it answers. A support assistant that reads the refund policy before it replies to a refund question is using RAG. An AI agent is an LLM that can take actions, such as booking a meeting or updating a support ticket, instead of only writing text. Fine-tuning means training an existing model further on your own examples, so it learns a style or a narrow task. You will not build these systems yourself, but you must know which one a problem needs.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 8, full screen]** A test sheet with columns: question, good answer, AI answer, pass or fail. The score at the bottom reads "190 out of 200 = 95%."

Skill three is evals, and I think it is the most important skill on this list. An eval, short for evaluation, is a test set for an AI product. You collect real questions, write down what a good answer looks like, and score the AI on each one before you ship. For the bank bot, the test set could be two hundred real questions about fees. If the bot gets one hundred ninety right, the score is ninety-five percent, and you decide whether ninety-five percent is good enough to launch.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 9, full screen]** An email inbox and a junk folder. Precision: "When it flags something, how often is it right?" Recall: "Of everything it should flag, how much does it catch?"

Evals are not only for senior jobs. The new graduate job I mentioned asks you to define evaluation frameworks with two common measures. Precision tells you how often the AI is right when it flags something. Recall tells you how much of the real problem it catches. Think of the spam filter in your email. When precision is high, real emails rarely land in the junk folder. When recall is high, junk mail rarely reaches your inbox.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 10, full screen]** Three labels: quality, speed, cost. Below them, the math: "1 cent × 1,000,000 questions a day = $10,000 a day = more than $3.5 million a year."

Skill four is making trade-offs between quality, speed, and cost. Speed here means latency, which is how long the user waits for a reply. Here is the math. Say each answer has a price of one cent. If your product handles one million questions a day, the bill is ten thousand dollars a day, or more than three and a half million dollars a year. A bigger model might be a little smarter and ten times more expensive. The AI product manager decides whether the smarter answers are worth the money. One senior job ad lists this exact duty: make hard trade-offs across quality, latency, cost, reliability, and user experience.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 11, full screen]** A short SQL query on screen: count the users who asked the bot about refunds last week. Beside it, an A/B test: half of the users see version A, half see version B, and the results are compared.

Skill five is working with data. An AI product runs on data, and an AI product manager who cannot read it must wait for someone else every time. SQL, or Structured Query Language, is the language people use to ask questions of a database. A question like "how many users asked the bot about refunds last week" is one short SQL query, and you should be able to write it yourself. You should also understand A/B tests. An A/B test shows the old version to half of your users and the new version to the other half, then compares the results.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 12, full screen]** A written description on the left, an arrow, and a simple working app on the right.

Skill six is building prototypes. A prototype is a rough first version of a product that people can click on. In 2026, AI coding tools such as Cursor, Claude Code, and Lovable can turn a written description into a simple working app. A working demo answers questions faster than a ten-page document, because people react to what they can touch.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]**

Many videos say you need no coding at all. For some AI product manager jobs, that advice is now out of date. One senior technical job ad I read asks every applicant to take a sixty-minute coding assessment. You do not need to become a software engineer, but you should be comfortable reading simple scripts and building small things with AI tools.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 13, full screen]** A one-page spec with four headings: the problem, the users, how we measure success, and "The AI must never." The last heading is highlighted.

Skill seven is the classic craft of the job: talking to users, choosing the most important problem, and writing a clear spec. A spec, also called a product requirements document or PRD, says what you are building, who it is for, and how you will know it worked. For an AI product, the PRD also lists what the AI must never do, such as give medical advice or show one customer's private data to another customer.

*[pause]*

## Part 4: The five phases

**[On camera]**

Knowing the skills is not enough, because you also need the order. If I had to become an AI product manager from zero today, this is exactly what I would do. The plan takes about six months at around ten hours a week. The weeks are only a guide. The test at the end of each phase is the real rule, so do not move on until you pass it.

**[Card beside you]** Phase 1: Learn how AI products work (weeks 1 to 4). Phase 2: Product and data basics (weeks 5 to 8). Phase 3: Evals and trade-offs (weeks 9 to 12). Phase 4: Create proof (weeks 13 to 20). Phase 5: Get hired (weeks 21 to 26).

If you already work as a product manager, you can move faster through phase two. If you already build AI systems as an engineer, you can move faster through phase one.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 14, full screen]** Phase 1 card. "Weeks 1 to 4. Learn how AI products work." The test: "Explain RAG, agents, and fine-tuning in two minutes."

Phase one is learning how AI products work, in weeks one to four. Use ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini every day for real tasks, and notice where each tool fails. Learn the terms from skills one and two: LLM, token, context window, hallucination, RAG, agent, and fine-tuning. The test for phase one is simple. You can explain to a friend, in two minutes, the difference between RAG, an agent, and fine-tuning, and when you would use each.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 15, full screen]** Phase 2 card. "Weeks 5 to 8. Product and data basics." The test: "Name the key metric, write the SQL query, draft the one-page spec."

Phase two is learning the product and data basics, in weeks five to eight. Draft a one-page spec for an AI feature in an app you already use. Learn enough SQL to filter, group, and join tables, and practice on a free public dataset. Learn how A/B tests and success metrics work. The test for phase two has three parts. You can name the most important metric for a product you use every day, write the SQL query that would measure it, and draft a one-page spec for one AI feature it should add.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 16, full screen]** Phase 3 card. "Weeks 9 to 12. Evals and trade-offs." The test: "A one-page table comparing two models on quality, cost, and speed."

Phase three is learning evals and trade-offs, in weeks nine to twelve. It comes before building on purpose, because every project you build later needs a quality score. Pick one AI feature, such as a tool that summarizes customer reviews. Build a test set of fifty real inputs, write what a good output looks like for each, and run two different models on all of them. Record the score, the price, and the waiting time for both. The test for phase three is a one-page table that compares the two models on quality, cost, and speed, along with a clear reason for the one you would ship.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 17, full screen]** Phase 4 card. "Weeks 13 to 20. Create proof." Three project tiles, each with four labels: problem, demo, eval score, cost per user.

Phase four is creating proof, in weeks thirteen to twenty. Build three small AI products with AI coding tools. Make three instead of one, because the first one will teach you the most and look the worst. Each project needs the problem it solves, a working demo, its eval score, and its cost per user. Publish every project on LinkedIn and GitHub, and get at least ten real people to use it. The test for phase four is simple. A stranger should be able to open your portfolio and understand the problem, the product, the score, and the cost in five minutes.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]** *(Opinion. Chris to confirm.)*

Phase five is getting hired, in weeks twenty-one to twenty-six. If you already have a job, look inside your own company first. Find the team that builds AI features, and offer to own their test set. An internal move skips the hardest part of hiring, which is getting a stranger to trust you. When you search outside your company, search for several titles, not one. The same job appears under names like Technical Product Manager for AI Systems, or Data and AI Product Manager.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 18, full screen]** Four interview areas, each with one sample question: product sense, technical depth, eval design, and metrics.

AI product manager interviews usually cover four areas. The first area is product sense: "Design an AI feature for a grocery app." The second is technical depth: "How would you reduce hallucinations?" The third is eval design: "How would you test this chatbot before launch?" The fourth is metrics: "Usage dropped ten percent. What do you check first?" Practice each one out loud at least five times. The test for phase five is an offer.

*[pause]*

## Your turn

**[On camera]**

Here is your task for this week. Pick an AI feature in an app you use every day. Write down ten real questions a user would ask it, and next to each question, write what a good answer looks like. You now have your first eval set, and the whole task takes about an hour.

A roadmap you watch does nothing for you. An eval set you build is your first piece of proof.
