## Part 1: The job

**[On camera]**

A normal product manager decides what gets built and why. That's the job.

An AI product manager does all of that, plus one more thing. They decide if the AI is good enough to ship.

**[Graphic 2, full screen]** Left: "Normal software: same question, same answer." Right: "AI: same question, different answer. Sometimes wrong."

*[pause]*

Because here's the problem with AI. Normal software? Same question, same answer, every time. AI? Same question, different answer. And sometimes that answer is wrong. Confidently wrong.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 3, full screen]** A bank chatbot. Monday: the right answer, with a green tick. Tuesday: the same question in different words, and an invented fee in red.

Picture a bank chatbot. Monday, someone asks about late fees. Perfect answer. Tuesday, someone asks the same thing in slightly different words. And the bot invents a fee that doesn't exist. Somebody has to catch that before customers do. That somebody is the AI product manager.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]**

So the job has two halves. Half one: what to build. Half two: is the AI good enough, what does it cost, and can you prove it. Most PMs have half one. Most engineers have half two. The people getting hired have both. That's the whole game.

**[On camera]**

Let's talk money. These are real job ads, posted in the US between August and October 2026. And these are base salaries. Not total pay. Base.

*[pause]*

Entry level, so associate and new grad: about one hundred twenty to one hundred eighty-five thousand. One new grad AI PM job at a big social media company pays one hundred twenty-two to one hundred eighty-four thousand. For a new grad. Smaller city, non-tech company? Closer to ninety.

Senior: about one hundred thirty to two hundred ten thousand. One senior job focused on AI agents and testing pays one hundred sixty-five to two hundred thousand. Fully remote.

Lead and principal is where it gets wide. Lowest I found: one hundred forty thousand. Highest: three hundred four thousand, for a principal PM building vision AI, which is AI that reads images and video. At a restaurant software company. Not an AI lab. A restaurant software company.

**[Graphic 5, full screen]** "Base: $304K max posted." Under it: "All US PMs, total pay median: $230K (Levels.fyi)."

And remember, that's base. Add bonus and stock, and Levels.fyi puts the median product manager in the US at about two hundred thirty thousand a year in total pay. That's all PMs, not just AI.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]**

Now here's what nobody tells you about those numbers. The jump from one twenty to three hundred isn't just years. It's the size of the decision they trust you with. Entry level? Help ship a feature. Principal? Decide if an AI system is safe, useful, and worth what it costs. You don't get paid for your time. You get paid for the decisions you can be trusted to make.

Outside the US, the numbers are lower. The skills are exactly the same.

## Part 3: The seven skills

**[On camera]**

Now the skills. I went through the job ads. Same skills, over and over. Seven of them.

**[Card beside you]** 1. LLMs. 2. RAG, agents, fine-tuning. 3. Evals. 4. Trade-offs. 5. Data. 6. Prototypes. 7. Specs.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 6, full screen]** A phone keyboard guessing the next word. Three labels: "Token: about 3/4 of a word." "Context window: how much it can read at once." "Hallucination: false, but confident."

One. Know how LLMs work. LLM means large language model. It's what's behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. All it does is predict the next word. Like your phone keyboard, just a lot better. Three words you need. Token: a chunk of text, about three quarters of a word. Every token costs money. Context window: how much the model can read at once. Hallucination: when it says something false with total confidence. Like our bank bot.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 7, full screen]** Three tiles. RAG: "Looks it up, then answers." Agent: "Does things, not just says things." Fine-tuning: "Trained on your examples."

Two. Know the three ways teams build with LLMs. RAG, retrieval augmented generation. The AI looks things up in your documents before it answers. A support bot that reads the refund policy before it answers a refund question? That's RAG. Agents. AI that does things, not just says things. Books the meeting. Updates the ticket. Fine-tuning. You train an existing model on your own examples so it learns a style or a narrow task. You won't build these. But if you can't tell which one a problem needs, you're not the PM. You're a spectator.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 8, full screen]** A test sheet: question, good answer, AI answer, pass or fail. At the bottom: "190 / 200 = 95%. Ship it?"

Three. Evals. If you only learn one skill from this video, make it this one. An eval is a test for your AI. You take real questions, write down what a good answer looks like, and score the AI on every single one. Bank bot. Two hundred real questions about fees. It gets one ninety right. That's ninety-five percent. Now, is ninety-five percent good enough to launch? That's your call. That's the job.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 9, full screen]** An inbox and a junk folder. Precision: "When it flags something, is it right?" Recall: "Did it catch everything it should?"

And this isn't just for senior people. A new grad AI PM job I found asks you to define evaluation frameworks using precision and recall. Precision: when the AI flags something, how often is it right? Recall: of everything it should have caught, how much did it catch? Think spam filter. High precision, your real emails don't end up in junk. High recall, junk doesn't end up in your inbox.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 10, full screen]** The math builds line by line. "1 cent × 1,000,000 a day = $10,000 a day." "= $3.65 million a year." "Bigger model, 10× the price = $36.5 million a year." Last line: "Worth it?"

Four. Trade-offs. Quality, speed, cost. You can't max all three. Let's do the math. Say every answer costs one cent. Sounds like nothing. Now you've got a million questions a day. That's ten thousand dollars a day. Three point six five million a year. Then someone says, "Let's use the bigger model. It's a bit smarter." It's also ten times the price. Now your bill is thirty-six and a half million a year. Is "a bit smarter" worth that? That's the question you get paid to answer. And I'm not making this up. A senior AI PM job ad lists it word for word: make hard trade-offs across quality, latency, cost, reliability, and user experience. Latency just means how long the user waits.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 11, full screen]** A short SQL query: users who asked about refunds last week. Next to it, an A/B split: half see version A, half see version B.

Five. Data. If you can't pull your own numbers, you wait on someone else. Every single time. Learn SQL, the language you use to ask a database questions. "How many users asked the bot about refunds last week?" That's one query. Write it yourself. Then learn A/B tests. Half your users get the old version, half get the new one, and you compare. That's how you prove something worked instead of guessing.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 12, full screen]** A written description, an arrow, and a working app.

Six. Build prototypes. A prototype is a rough version people can actually click on. Today, tools like Cursor, Claude Code, and Lovable turn a written description into a working app. A demo beats a ten-page document every time, because people react to what they can touch.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]**

And I'm going to be straight with you. A lot of videos tell you that you don't need any coding. For some of these jobs, that's no longer true. One senior AI PM job I read makes every applicant take a sixty-minute coding test. You don't need to be an engineer. But you can't be scared of code either.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 13, full screen]** A one-page spec. The last heading, "The AI must never," is highlighted.

Seven. The basics that never go away. Talk to users. Pick the problem that matters most. Write a clear spec. That's a PRD, a product requirements document: what you're building, who it's for, and how you'll know it worked. And for AI, add one more section: what the AI must never do. Never give medical advice. Never show one customer another customer's data. That one section can save a company.

*[pause]*

## Part 4: The five phases

**[On camera]**

Now the order. Because the right skills in the wrong order go nowhere. If I was starting from zero today, this is exactly what I'd do. About six months, about ten hours a week. But the weeks don't matter. The tests do. Pass the test, move on. Fail it, stay. No exceptions.

**[Card beside you]** Phase 1: Learn how AI works. Phase 2: Product and data basics. Phase 3: Evals and trade-offs. Phase 4: Create proof. Phase 5: Get hired.

Already a PM? Move fast through phase two. Already an engineer building AI? Move fast through phase one.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 14, full screen]** Phase 1. Weeks 1 to 4. Test: "Explain RAG, agents, and fine-tuning in 2 minutes."

Phase one. Weeks one to four. Learn how AI products work. Use ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini every day on real tasks, and notice where each one breaks. Learn the words: LLM, token, context window, hallucination, RAG, agent, fine-tuning. The test? Explain RAG, agents, and fine-tuning to a friend in two minutes, and when you'd use each. Can't do it in two minutes? You don't know it yet.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 15, full screen]** Phase 2. Weeks 5 to 8. Test: "Name the metric. Write the query. Write the spec."

Phase two. Weeks five to eight. Product and data basics. Write a one-page spec for an AI feature in an app you already use. Learn enough SQL to filter, group, and join tables. Practice on a free public dataset. Learn A/B tests and success metrics. The test? Pick an app you use every day. Name its most important metric. Write the query that measures it. Write a one-page spec for one AI feature it should add. All three, or you're not done.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 16, full screen]** Phase 3. Weeks 9 to 12. Test: "One page. Two models. Quality, cost, speed. Which one ships?"

Phase three. Weeks nine to twelve. Evals and trade-offs. And yes, this comes before building. On purpose. Because everything you build next needs a score. Pick one AI feature, say a tool that summarizes customer reviews. Build a test set of fifty real inputs. Write what good looks like for each one. Run two different models on all fifty. Track the score, the price, and the speed. The test? One page. Two models. Quality, cost, speed. Which one you'd ship, and why.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 17, full screen]** Phase 4. Weeks 13 to 20. Three project tiles, each showing: problem, demo, eval score, cost per user.

Phase four. Weeks thirteen to twenty. Create proof. Build three small AI products. Not one. Three. The first one teaches you the most and looks the worst. That's fine. That's the point. Every project shows four things: the problem, a working demo, its eval score, and its cost per user. Put them on LinkedIn and GitHub. Get ten real people to use each one. The test? A stranger opens your portfolio and gets the problem, the product, the score, and the cost in five minutes. Nobody hires potential. They hire proof.

*[pause]*

**[On camera]** *(Opinion. Chris to confirm.)*

Phase five. Weeks twenty-one to twenty-six. Get hired. Already have a job? Start inside your own company. Find the team building AI features and offer to own their evals. An internal move skips the hardest part of hiring, which is getting a stranger to trust you. Searching outside? Don't search one title. The same job shows up as Technical Product Manager for AI Systems, Data and AI Product Manager, and more. Search them all.

*[pause]*

**[Graphic 18, full screen]** Four interview questions, one per area: product sense, technical, evals, metrics.

Then get ready for four kinds of questions. Product sense: "Design an AI feature for a grocery app." Technical: "How would you reduce hallucinations?" Evals: "How would you test this chatbot before launch?" Metrics: "Usage dropped ten percent. What do you check first?" Practice each one out loud five times. Not in your head. Out loud. The test for phase five? An offer.

*[pause]*

## Your turn

**[On camera]**

Here's what I want you to do this week. Not next month. This week. Pick an AI feature in an app you use every day. Write down ten real questions someone would ask it. Next to each one, write what a good answer looks like. Congratulations. You just built your first eval set. It takes about an hour.

Most people who watch this won't do it. That's exactly why the ones who do get hired.
