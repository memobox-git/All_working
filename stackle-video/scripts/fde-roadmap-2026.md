### What all of them cover (our script covers this too)

- **The definition:** an engineer who works with customers after the sale and writes production code for them.
- **The origin:** Palantir.
- **Who is hiring:** OpenAI, Anthropic and other AI companies building FDE teams.
- **A skills list:**
  - Python and APIs
  - cloud and DevOps
  - LLMs, RAG and agents
  - customer communication
- **Portfolio projects,** usually a RAG app and an agent.
- **Salary,** often as the main hook.

### Where they are wrong, out of date, or disagree

- **"$1M job" framing (videos 5 and 6).** A small number of senior roles at top AI labs can reach that range, mostly in stock, so it is not typical pay. Typical pay:
  - The median advertised FDE salary is more than $188,000 (Lightcast, via Fortune).
  - The Levels.fyi median total pay is $216,250.
- **Microsoft "6,000 FDE hires" (video 1, Telegram post).** Microsoft's Frontier Co. pools about 6,000 existing staff: FDEs, consultants, industry specialists and salespeople. It is not 6,000 new FDE hires. **The script leaves this out.**
- **Timelines disagree.** Video 7 promises 90 days, video 3 promises 6 months, and written guides say 12 to 18 months for new graduates. What the job posts actually show: only 12% of FDE postings ask for 0 to 2 years of experience, and 60% ask for 3 to 5 years (Bloomberry). The script says this plainly.
- **Growth numbers get mixed up.** Each of these uses a different dataset and period, so never combine them:
  - "800%" is the FT and Indeed figure for Jan to Sep 2025.
  - "729%" is Indeed via Business Insider, April 2025 to April 2026.
  - "1,165%" is Bloomberry, 2025 vs 2024.
  - "More than 1,000%" is Lightcast via Fortune, Jan to Aug 2026 vs 2025.
  - "5,000%" and "5,230%" figures also circulate, but their baseline is garbled. Avoid them.
- **Stale call to action (video 3).** Its bootcamp started Oct 4, 2026.

### Gaps, and the angle this script takes

1. **Every number has a source,** and the script says so on camera. This answers the $1M hype directly.
2. **The job is defined by accountability for the customer's result, not by a tool list.** The idea comes from Natalie Meurer's 2026 talk. None of the roadmap videos frame the job this way.
3. **Palantir's Dev, Delta and Echo split is explained clearly.** The other videos mention Palantir but do not explain it.
4. **The experience question gets an honest answer.** The FDE role is usually a second or third job, and a 90-day plan only works if you already have engineering experience.
5. **The roadmap puts data before AI.** Data and cloud come before applied AI. This is your data engineering angle, and it matches what job posts and the MIT study say about where projects fail.
6. **Projects are built like real deployments.** Each one has a real user, is put online, has an eval score and ends with a one-page case study.
7. **The interview section includes your hiring experience.** It also warns about jobs that use the FDE title for consulting work.

### PART 1. WHAT A FORWARD DEPLOYED ENGINEER DOES

**[Graphic 3, full screen]** Part title card: "Part 1: What the job is".

> Before you learn a single skill, you need a clear picture of the job itself, because the title confuses a lot of people. So the first part of this video explains what a forward deployed engineer is, where the job came from, and why companies suddenly want so many of them.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 4, full screen]** Split screen. Left: one product box sending lines out to hundreds of small user dots. Right: one engineer icon sitting inside a single company building, wiring the product into that company's data, systems and people.

> A forward deployed engineer, or FDE, is a software developer who works inside one customer's business to make a product work there. Most developers build one product for thousands of users and rarely meet any of them. An FDE sits with a single client, learns how its people work, and writes the code that connects the software to their data, their systems, and their daily tasks.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 5, full screen]** Timeline dot lands on "2006". A Palantir wordmark appears, then "Shyam Sankar, employee #13".

> The FDE job started at Palantir, a software company that helps governments and large businesses use their data. Shyam Sankar, who joined the company in 2006 as its thirteenth employee, is credited with creating the role.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 6, full screen]** Two columns build in. Left: "Devs: built the core product". Right: "Deltas: made it work for each customer". Then an "Echo: knew the customer's industry" card slides in next to "Deltas" and links to it.

> Palantir split its engineering team into two groups. The people who built the core product were called Devs, and the people who went out to customers and made the product work for them were known as Deltas. Bob McGrew, a former Palantir executive, has explained that each Delta worked next to a partner in a role called Echo, who knew the customer's industry while the Delta wrote the code.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 7, full screen]** An "LLM" card in the centre. Three green ticks appear: "write", "summarize", "answer questions". Then three locked doors appear: "company files", "company rules", "daily software".

> For years, the FDE job was mostly a Palantir idea. The idea spread when AI companies began selling large language models to businesses. A large language model, or LLM, is the type of AI behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, and it learns to write by reading huge amounts of text. An LLM can write, summarize, and answer questions, but on its own it cannot open a company's files, follow its rules, or plug into the software its staff use every day.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 8, full screen]** A grid of 100 dots. 95 fade to grey and 5 glow amber. Caption: "MIT, 2025".

> Many businesses bought AI tools and then struggled to make them useful. In 2025, a research group at MIT looked at company AI projects and found that about ninety-five out of every hundred companies it studied got no measurable return from their AI pilots, which are small test projects. The researchers blamed the way the tools fit into daily work, not the quality of the AI models.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 9, full screen]** Company names drop in one by one, starting with OpenAI and a "January 2025" tag, then "$4B+ deployment company, May 2026". Then "Salesforce: 1,000 FDEs", then Anthropic, Databricks, Scale AI, Ramp, Harvey and Glean.

> AI companies responded by hiring FDEs to close the gap between the model and the business. OpenAI started a forward deployed engineering team in January 2025, and in May 2026 it launched a separate company focused only on deployment, backed by more than four billion dollars. Salesforce has committed to building a team of one thousand FDEs, and Anthropic, Databricks, Scale AI, Ramp, Harvey, and Glean all hire for the role.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 10, full screen]** Flow diagram inside a bank building. "Files" connect to "AI model", then "review screen", then "checked against the rules", then "staff use it every day". Each step lights up as it is spoken.

> Banking gives a clear example of the work. Imagine a bank that buys an AI model to help its staff review loan applications. The model can read a document, but it cannot open the bank's files, it does not know the lending rules, and no one on the team knows how to check whether its answers are right. An FDE connects the AI to the files, builds a review screen for the staff, checks the answers against the rules, and stays until the team uses it every day.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 11, full screen]** Large text: "Judged by the result, not the code." Below it, a meter labelled "loan reviews: faster, more accurate" fills up. Credit line: "Natalie Meurer, Sierra, AI Engineer World's Fair 2026".

> An FDE is judged by the customer's result, not by the code. Natalie Meurer from Sierra, a company that builds AI for customer service, made this point in a 2026 talk at the AI Engineer World's Fair. She said the FDE title now covers so many different jobs that it can mean almost nothing, and that what truly defines the role is accountability to the customer. For the bank, the work is not finished when the code runs. The work is finished when loan reviews are faster or more accurate, and the bank can measure the difference.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 12, full screen]** A four-column comparison table builds one column at a time: Software engineer, Solutions engineer, Consultant, FDE. Three rows: "When", "Delivers", "Measured by".

> People often mix up the FDE with three other jobs, so it helps to see them side by side. A software engineer builds one product for many customers. A solutions engineer works before the sale and builds demos that help a customer decide to buy. A consultant studies a company's problem and usually delivers advice and a plan. An FDE works after the sale, writes production code, which is code that real people depend on every day, and owns the result.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 13, full screen]** A job post card with "FDE" in the title. A magnifying glass passes over it and highlights two checks: "writes real code?" and "owns a real result?".

> Job titles can hide what a role actually involves, so read every FDE posting closely. The Pragmatic Engineer, a popular newsletter for software engineers, noted in 2026 that some roles with the FDE title now look more like consulting or setup work. Before you apply, check whether the job involves writing real code and owning a real result.

> Now that you know what an FDE does, you can ask the more important question, which is whether the role is right for you. In my view, the FDE job suits a specific kind of engineer. Neither path is better than the other, but you should know which one fits you before you spend months preparing. ### PART 3. THE ROADMAP IN FIVE PHASES

**[Graphic 19, full screen]** Part title card: "Part 3: The roadmap". Five numbered blocks appear in a row and stay as a progress bar at the top for the rest of Part 3: 1 Software foundations, 2 Data and cloud, 3 Applied AI, 4 Customer skills, 5 Proof of work.

> The five phases are software foundations, data and cloud, applied AI, customer skills, and proof of work.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 20, full screen]** Block 1 lights up. Three cards: "Python: in about 2 of 3 FDE job posts", "SQL", and "API". SQL shows a short query returning "orders shipped last month". API shows a weather app sending a request to a weather service and getting "today's forecast" back.

> Phase one is software foundations, because an FDE is an engineer who ships code before anything else. Start with Python, the programming language that appears in about two thirds of FDE job posts. Then learn SQL, the language you use to ask a database for data, such as every order a store shipped last month. Next, learn to build and call an API, short for application programming interface, which is the way one program asks another program for something, like a weather app asking a weather service for today's forecast.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 21, full screen]** Two smaller cards. "Git": a timeline of saved changes with an undo arrow. "TypeScript: in about 1 of 3 FDE job posts": a simple screen being built.

> Phase one also includes two smaller tools. Git is the tool that tracks every change to your code, so you can undo mistakes and work with a team. TypeScript is a language used to build web pages and apps, and about a third of FDE job posts mention it, because customers need a simple screen to use what you build.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 22, full screen]** Block 2 lights up. Scattered sources (an old database, spreadsheets, PDF files, some marked with red errors) flow through a pipe labelled "data pipeline". On the other side they come out as one clean table. Example label: "hospital: new patient records, every night, duplicates removed".

> Phase two is data and cloud, because your code is only useful once it can reach the customer's information. In my experience, this is also where real projects slow down the most. When you arrive at a customer, the information you need is usually spread across old databases, spreadsheets, and PDF files, and some of it is wrong. Your first weeks often go into getting access, fixing the errors, and moving everything to a place where the AI can use it. The code that does this moving and cleaning is called a data pipeline. For example, a pipeline at a hospital might pull new patient records every night, remove the duplicates, and store them in one clean table.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 23, full screen]** Code and its parts drop into a box labelled "Docker container". The box lifts onto a cloud with three labels: AWS, Google Cloud, Azure. An "AWS: most mentioned in FDE job posts" tag highlights the AWS label.

> The second half of phase two is the cloud, which means renting computers from companies like Amazon, Google, or Microsoft instead of running your own. Learn Docker first, a tool that packs your code and everything it needs into one container that runs the same way on any computer. Then learn one provider, such as AWS, Google Cloud, or Azure, well enough to put your Docker container online. AWS appears in more FDE job posts than any other provider, so it is a safe first choice.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 24, full screen]** A lock icon labelled "access control". Two quick panels: a hospital record that the wrong person cannot open, and a bank account that another client cannot see.

> Phase two ends with access control, which decides who is allowed to see what. A hospital tool must never show one patient's records to the wrong person, and a bank tool must never show one client's account to another. Large companies check access rules before anything goes live, so you need to know how logins and permissions work.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 25, full screen]** Block 3 lights up. An app sends a message to an "LLM API". A prompt card fills in three lines: "who the assistant is helping", "what to answer", "what to say when you do not know".

> Phase three is applied AI, which comes third because a model can only be as good as the information you give it. Applied AI means building with models that already exist instead of training your own. Start by calling an LLM through its API and learning to write clear instructions for it, which is called prompting. For example, a good prompt says who the assistant is helping, what to answer, and what to say when it does not know.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 26, full screen]** "RAG" flow. A question ("How many leave days do I have left?") goes to "find the right documents", which pulls "leave policy" from a document stack. The question and the policy then go to the model, which gives an answer.

> Next, learn retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. It means the system first finds the right documents and then hands them to the model, so the model answers from the company's own facts instead of from memory. For example, an assistant that answers employee questions can look up the leave policy before it tells someone how many days off they have left.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 27, full screen]** "Agent" at a car parts supplier. Three tool icons light up in order: "check stock", "place order", "send confirmation email".

> After RAG, learn agents. An agent is an AI system that takes actions instead of only writing text, by calling tools such as a search engine, a database, or an email service. For example, an agent at a car parts supplier could check stock, place an order, and send a confirmation email, one step at a time.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 28, full screen]** A single plug labelled "MCP (Anthropic, November 2024)" connects one tool to several different AI apps at once. Tag: "Anthropic FDE job posts: build MCP servers".

> Many agents now connect to their tools through the Model Context Protocol, or MCP. Anthropic introduced it in November 2024 as an open standard, which means anyone can use it for free. The protocol works like one common plug, so a tool built once can connect to many different AI apps. Anthropic's own FDE job posts list building these connectors, called MCP servers, as part of the work.

**[PAUSE]**

**[Graphic 29, full screen]** A "change prompt" button. Before the change goes through, a test runs "100 known questions" and shows a score: before 91, after 93, with a green tick. Tag: "OpenAI and Anthropic FDE job posts mention evaluation".

> The last skill in phase three is evals, short for evaluations, and both OpenAI and Anthropic name this skill in their FDE job posts. An eval is a set of test questions with known correct answers that you run every time you change the system, so you can prove it got better and not worse. For example, before you change the prompt in a hospital assistant, you run one hundred known questions and check that the score did not drop. Customers believe a measured result more than a demo, and this is how you give them one.

The third is explaining trade-offs in plain words, such as telling a manager that faster answers will cost more money per question. The fourth is writing, because short and clear updates keep people's trust when you are not in the room.
