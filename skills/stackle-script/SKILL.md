# Stackle YouTube script rules

Use these rules every time you write, rewrite or review a video script for Chris and the channel "Stackle - Your Career Lab". They were built from his corrections across many scripts. When a rule here conflicts with a general writing habit, the rule here wins. When Chris gives a new instruction in the conversation, his instruction wins over this file.

## 1. Who is speaking and who is watching

- The speaker is Chris, founder of Stackle (stackle.io). He has worked in data engineering for more than ten years, has interviewed more than 200 engineers for companies in healthcare, banking, automotive and financial exchanges, and has more than 200,000 followers on LinkedIn.
- The viewer wants a job as a data engineer or an AI engineer. Many are students, new graduates or working professionals changing fields. Many speak English as a second language.
- The channel teaches the skills that get people hired and sells two things: a free 15-minute career call, and the Stackle masterclasses (Data Engineering and AI Engineering).
- Never mention Chris's employer, any company he works at, internal work, or any "at [company]" framing. Use industries instead of company names.
- Never use the word "candidate". Say "engineer", "person" or "you".

## 2. Research before you write

Do this every time Chris asks for a new script, before writing a single line. A small edit to an existing script does not need it.

1. **Find the top creators for the topic.** Search YouTube for the topic in Chris's Google Chrome, using the Claude in Chrome tools. He keeps Chrome open. Stay inside his niche, data engineering and AI engineering. Pick 5 to 8 videos: the most viewed, the most recent (the last 12 to 18 months), and the ones from the biggest creators in the niche.
2. **Flag every creator.** For each video, record the creator, the channel, the title, the link, the views, the upload date and the length.
3. **Read their scripts.** Open each video's transcript (under the description, "Show transcript") and read the text. If a video has no transcript, use its title, description, chapters and top comments, and say that the transcript was missing.
4. **Map what they said.** For each video, note the hook, the order of the parts, the main points, the examples and every number with its source. Read the top comments for questions viewers still have and things they complain about.
5. **Fact-check.** Check every number and claim the script will use against a primary source, such as official documentation, government statistics, company reports or large surveys. Flag any place where the creators are wrong, out of date, or disagree with each other. Never repeat a creator's mistake.
6. **Choose our version.** Content that is similar to other videos is fine, because most topics overlap. The rule is that no part of the script should be something another creator has already explained in a better way. For each part, either explain it more clearly and simply than the best version out there, or add what the others missed: Chris's hiring experience, newer facts, a better example, or a question from the comments. Do not go off-topic just to be different.
7. **Never copy.** Use other scripts only to compare. Do not reuse their sentences, hooks or examples. Write in Chris's words, following the rules in this file.

Hand over a short research brief in the same reply, before the script:

- A table of the creators and videos you flagged.
- What all of them cover. The script must cover this too.
- Where they are wrong, out of date, or disagree.
- The gaps, and the angle our script takes.
- The source for every number.

Then write the script straight away. Do not wait for Chris to approve the brief. If Chrome is not connected, tell Chris and ask whether to use web search instead.

## 3. The order of every video

1. **Hook.** The key fact or the problem, in the first sentence.
2. **Promise.** "In this video I am going to…" followed by what the viewer will learn, in plain words. Say this phrase once in the whole script.
3. **Intro.** Chris introduces himself after the promise, not before it. See section 4.
4. **The parts.** Each part teaches one thing. Tell the viewer the parts in the order they will come.
5. **Your turn.** One thing the viewer can do this week.
6. **Call to action.** The free 15-minute call first, then the masterclass. Then one question for the comments.

## 4. The intro

It comes after the promise and starts with "If you are new here, I am Chris." Each sentence is one fact, so that each fact can get its own picture on screen.

Standard version, used for roadmap, hiring and career videos:

> If you are new here, I am Chris. I have worked in data engineering for more than ten years. I have interviewed more than two hundred engineers for companies in healthcare, banking, automotive, and financial exchanges. Today I run Stackle, where I teach data and AI engineering, and more than two hundred thousand people follow me on LinkedIn.

For a video that explains a tool or a concept (RAG, SQL, Airflow, agents), ask Chris whether he wants the interviews sentence in. He has cut hiring talk from tool videos before. If he says no, drop that one sentence and keep the rest.

Do not talk about hiring inside the teaching parts of a tool video. One line near the end, about what gets a person an interview, is fine.

## 5. The hook

- Put the key fact first. Do not build up to it.
- Keep it to one or two sentences. If the same idea is said twice, cut one.
- Use a number or a concrete situation the viewer recognises.
- Only use numbers that have a source. If Chris gives a number that has no source, say so and offer the nearest number that does.
- Use "can" when something happens only sometimes. For example, "it can answer twenty days", because a viewer may test it and get a different result.

Bad: a paragraph that describes the job market before giving the salary.
Good: "An AI engineer in the United States now earns between one hundred seventy thousand dollars and more than a million dollars a year."

## 6. Sentence rules

These are the rules Chris enforces most often.

1. **Simple English.** A student in the 8th grade must be able to follow every sentence. Use short common words.
2. **Complete sentences.** Every sentence has a subject and says a whole thought. No fragments.
3. **No abrupt starts or endings.** A paragraph must not begin as if the viewer already knows what it is about, and must not stop in the middle of an idea.
4. **Name the subject.** Each paragraph must make sense if the viewer heard nothing before it. Do not open a paragraph with "it", "that", "this", "the fix", "the problem" or "one". Write "become an AI engineer", never "become one". Write "teach you RAG", never "fix that problem".
5. **Do not repeat the name either.** Name the subject once at the start of a paragraph, then use "it" while the meaning is clear. No keyword more than twice in one paragraph.
6. **Say each thing once.** No paragraph may repeat the idea of the paragraph before it.
7. **Name the things.** Give the real technical name and then a simple explanation. "The convolutional neural network, or CNN, which is built for images." Never replace names with "the first one, the second one".
8. **Explain every term the first time.** If the script says "context window", "embedding" or "LLM", it explains the term in the same sentence or uses plain words instead.
9. **Give an example for each concept.** One short, real-life example per idea, such as a phone finishing a sentence or an inbox catching spam.
10. **No invented people.** Do not create a character with a name and follow their story. Speak to the viewer as "you".
11. **No long running example that takes over the script.** A single example can return at the end to close the loop (the leave-days question does this in the RAG script). An extended made-up business, such as an online store, is not allowed.
12. **No metaphor that runs through the script.** No "climb", "climbing", "step up", "summit", "journey". Say "the next phase" and "move on to".
13. **Tell it as a story means flow.** Each part leads to the next with a reason. It does not mean adding a made-up story.
14. **Do not lecture.** No moral lessons, no "you must understand that".
15. **Do not make it longer unless asked.** When fixing one part, change that part only.
16. **Stay faithful to a source.** When Chris gives a transcript or outline to work from, keep its structure and facts. Mark anything you added.

## 7. Words and phrases never to use

- "no fluff", "fluff", "no sponsor", "no agenda"
- "let's get into it", "let's dive in", "in today's video"
- "game changer"
- "make sure you hit subscribe", "don't forget to like and subscribe", "smash that subscribe button"
- "candidate"
- "myth" where "wrong idea" is clearer
- Any emoji

## 8. Other AI products

When a script names ChatGPT, Gemini or another product, describe it on its own merits. Do not make one named product look bad when the limit is true of every model. If a limit applies to all of them, say so: "ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini each run on a large language model."

## 9. Length and pace

- Chris speaks about 150 words a minute. A 10-minute video is about 1,500 words.
- State the word count and the estimated minutes at the top of every script.
- Put only one idea in a sentence that will be spoken. Split a sentence that carries three ideas.

## 10. Marking the script for filming

Every paragraph gets one of three labels, and about half of the video should be full-screen graphics.

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Graphic N, full screen | The graphic fills the screen and the voice continues. Write one line that says what it shows. |
| Card beside you | Chris stays on screen and a card sits next to him. Used for lists such as ten patterns. |
| On camera | Only Chris. Used for the intro bridge, the lines between parts, opinions and the call to action. |

- Anything the viewer has to picture becomes a graphic: a process, a comparison, a list of names, a number.
- Anything that is Chris giving an opinion or asking for something stays on camera.
- Mark a one-second **pause** wherever a graphic starts or ends. Chris records in one take and pauses at the marks.
- End the script with a numbered list of the graphics and what each one shows.
- On-screen text must use the words of the script. If you shorten or invent a label, tell Chris which ones.

## 11. Title and description

Title: put the phrase people search for at the front, and keep it under 70 characters. Example: "AI Engineer Roadmap 2026: How to Become an AI Engineer From Zero".

Description, in this order:
1. Two or three sentences that repeat the hook and the promise.
2. Chapters, starting at 0:00, one per part. Leave the times for Chris to fill in after the edit.
3. The free call link and the masterclass link.
4. Sources for any numbers.
5. This exact last line:

```
Book a Call — https://calendar.app.google/2HjLnF1HSov9m5Gk9 | LinkedIn — https://www.linkedin.com/in/crispusroshan | DE Roadmap — https://stackle.io/resources/data-engineering-roadmap | DE Masterclass — https://stackle.io/courses/data-engineering-masterclass-get-job-ready-in-10-weeks | Meetup — https://www.meetup.com/stackle-your-career-lab
```

## 12. How to work with Chris

- Start writing. Do not send a plan and wait. Do the research in section 2 first, then send the research brief and the script together.
- When he asks for one change, make that change and show the new lines. Do not resend the whole script unless he asks.
- After every edit, confirm the edit really landed in the file before sending it.
- After fixing one fault, check the whole script for the same fault. Then check that the fix did not create the opposite fault (for example, fixing vague references by repeating one word in every sentence).
- If you added facts, examples or names that he did not give you, list them so he can check them.
- If he gives a number or claim you cannot support, say so plainly and offer what you can support.
- Visuals use the Plus Jakarta Sans font and the Stackle colours from stackle.io: blue-to-cyan accent (#2563EB → #10A5BE) on white (#FFFFFF, text #171717) for stills, or #3B82F6 → #22D3EE on near-black #0A0A0A (text #FAFAFA) for video. Never use amber/gold (#F59E0B, #BA7517), charcoal #1E1C1D or cream #FAF8F1.

## 13. Check before you hand over a script

Read the finished script once and answer each question. Fix anything that fails.

1. Does the first sentence give the key fact or the problem?
2. Does "In this video I am going to" appear once, and does the promise use plain words?
3. Is the intro after the promise, starting with "If you are new here, I am Chris"?
4. Does every paragraph make sense on its own, with its subject named?
5. Is any word repeated more than twice in one paragraph?
6. Does any paragraph repeat the one before it?
7. Is every technical term named and explained the first time?
8. Does every concept have one short example?
9. Is there any invented person, any long made-up example, or any running metaphor?
10. Is any phrase from section 7 in the script?
11. Is any employer or company Chris works at mentioned?
12. Does every number have a source?
13. Are the word count and minutes at the top?
14. Is every paragraph labelled graphic, card or on camera, with the pauses marked?
15. Does the description end with the exact links line?
16. Was the research in section 2 done, with the top creators flagged in the brief, and does every part of the script match or beat the best version already on YouTube?
