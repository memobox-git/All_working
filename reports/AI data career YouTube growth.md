# Data career channels needed 16 months, not 6

No YouTube channel in the data and AI career niche has a verified path from zero to 100,000 subscribers in six months. The fastest verified paths took **16 to 20 months**. Marina Wyss (AI and machine learning careers) posted about one long video a week and held her "100K" video at month 16. Akber Shaikh (Indian tech-career roadmaps) passed 100K within 17.6 months. Ansh Lamba (data engineering full courses, one video every Sunday, zero Shorts) passed it before about month 20. Only Nate Herk, in the nearby AI-automation niche, claims faster growth: **230,000 subscribers in nine months**, after he quit his job and posted two to three long videos a week. The winners shared a few simple habits. They kept a fixed weekly slot for long videos. They had one search-friendly video take off early. They used formats that answer a career fear ("how I'd start over", "don't do this", "tester to data engineer", interview questions, free full courses). And they put their proof in the title. Shorts brought reach, but turned viewers into subscribers at about one-third the rate of long videos. A big LinkedIn audience did not move to YouTube by itself; a free, dated bootcamp event did. The business model closest to Stackle, TrendyTech, sells a ₹79,000 program through a free guidance call and needed about five years to reach 100K on YouTube. So Chris's business does not need 100K subscribers to work. The playbook at the end turns this into one Hormozi-style long video a week (two in the launch months), three to five Shorts a week, daily Reels and LinkedIn posts, a free bootcamp every quarter, a simple funnel (Short → long video → free guide → 15-minute call → Masterclass), and a lean tool stack of about $25–45 a month.

A simple sum shows why six months is so hard. Reaching 100K in six months needs about **17,000 new subscribers every month, starting in month one**. ## One fixed weekly long video was the habit every winner shared

The fast data channels did not post long videos every day. They posted at least once a week and did not miss. Ansh Lamba uploaded **every Sunday**, about 53 videos in his first year, and posted zero Shorts ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@AnshLambaJSR/videos)). Rishabh Mishra posted about 1.1 a week in his first year. Darshil Parmar posted about 1.1 a week for two years ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@DarshilParmar)). Marina Wyss posted about one a week for nearly two years ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@MarinaWyssAI)). The faster growers did more. Ankit Bansal posted about **three SQL interview videos a week** in his first six months ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@ankitbansal6)). Baraa posted two to three a week by cutting one big course into numbered chapters. Nate Herk posted two to three a week, then about five ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@nateherk)). Many fast growers did this while holding full-time jobs: Rishabh as a senior data analyst in Germany ([site](https://www.rishabhnmishra.com/)), Akber as a senior engineer at JPMorgan Chase ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@MediocreKuchKar)), and Marina at Twitch until she quit in September 2026 ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yw9mRO6t1MI)).

The clearest record of a daily routine comes from Jeff Su (1.92M subscribers), who built his channel while working at Google. For two years he worked on YouTube from about **6:30 pm to 11 pm (sometimes later) on weeknights and 8 am to 8 pm on weekends**, with a rule of one video a week ([Ali Abdaal](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LLjpnubsOWc)). Each video took about 20 hours: **8–10 hours of scripting, about 1 hour of filming, and 4–5 hours** briefing and reviewing his editor. He would not start a script until the title and thumbnail were fixed, and he says "hook is more important than the script outline". He scripts every word. He saves ideas in Todoist the moment they come. He briefs his editor with Canva mock-ups. Before weddings or busy weeks, he filmed ahead. His advice to new creators: "Find someone you respect… Study them. Copy them."

Other daily habits show up in the record. Zach Wilson "created content on LinkedIn every day for a year" before he knew it was his new life ([Avery Smith interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lNxDjCDVC54)). Sajjaad Khader posted **two or more Shorts a day** in his 2023 growth phase, and he now has 1,208 Shorts ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SajjaadKhader/shorts)). Satish Dhawale ran a daily "30 Days SQL learning Challenge", and its first episode has **978K views** ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KvT6urWNBBc)). CampusX's biggest video comes from its numbered "100 Days of Machine Learning" series and has 3.21M views ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZftI2fEz0Fw)).

Not everyone worked 50 hours a week. Ali Abdaal's template for people with a full-time job is about **8 hours a week for one video**: about 2 hours on the idea, title and thumbnail, 1 hour on the script, 30 minutes filming and 30 minutes reviewing the edit, with editing outsourced ([Recall summary](https://www.recall.it/summary/ali-abdaal/if-i-started-youtube-while-working-full-time-id-do-this)). Sundas Khalid says she spends about **4–5 hours a week**, helped by a virtual assistant and a team of editors ([LTN](https://ec.ltn.com.tw/article/breakingnews/5201342)). The common point is clear. The creator spends their own hours on ideas, titles and scripts, and pays someone else to edit.

Alex Hormozi's lessons fit Chris's style closely. Over about 40 months, Hormozi says he made 35,000 pieces of content and spent $4M ([Hormozi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jmkq5RLjm0U)). He names six shifts: from edutainment to education, from "for us" to "for you", from wide to narrow, from views to revenue, from Shorts to long videos, and from "assume more" to "assume nothing". He says an education video's intro must prove you can deliver what the thumbnail promised. He found that spending a quarter of the old editing time on research before filming removed **90–95% of the editing work**. He has since dropped colour grading, and an iPhone video shot on a plane became one of his best ([Hormozi, Aug 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZTSI3DDP_4A)). To start, he paid a basic agency about $3–5K a month whose main value was making him commit to **three videos a week** for 3–6 months. He re-posts old winners, because new viewers have not seen them, and he titles videos "like they don't know you". In his team he asks for updates every day, not every week, and ends every meeting with "who, what, when".

One gap matters here. We found no first-person daily routine from the Indian data creators (Ansh Lamba, Rishabh Mishra, Akber Shaikh, Darshil Parmar, Ankit Bansal). Their journey videos exist, but they could not be transcribed. So most of the routine evidence comes from US creators.

## Shorts buy reach, but long videos win subscribers and buyers

Shorts and long videos do different jobs. Across the channels we measured, Shorts-led channels earned about **5–8.5 subscribers per 1,000 lifetime views**, while long-video channels earned about **15–27** (our estimate from verified totals; for example [Sajjaad Khader](https://www.youtube.com/@SajjaadKhader) and [Marina Wyss](https://www.youtube.com/@MarinaWyssAI)). An older study of 5,400 Shorts by Paddy Galloway found 1.69 subscribers per 1,000 Shorts views against 2.27 for long videos ([Adam Connell](https://adamconnell.me/youtube-shorts-statistics/)). Hormozi says "longs drive a lot more conversions than shorts", meaning book sales, opt-ins and applications ([Hormozi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jmkq5RLjm0U)). He also says "someone would have to watch 480 shorts to have the same level of exposure as watching 2 hours with me" ([Hormozi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dMZ-n2KSlxE)). Shorts-heavy creators earn mainly from brand deals, not courses. Sundas Khalid claims **$302,000** from her content business in 2024, mostly brand partnerships ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/ive-grown-income-700-600k-155555331.html)). And three fast channels, Ansh Lamba, CodeHead and Dave Ebbelaar, posted zero Shorts.

Still, Shorts help when they support long videos. YouTube says the two formats use separate recommendation systems, and it has seen no evidence that Shorts hurt long videos ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/debunking-common-myths-about-youtube-shorts/)). A 2026 study of 18,000 channels found that education channels that mixed in Shorts had **37% more median subscribers** than long-only channels at the same view level. The best results came when Shorts were **25–40% of uploads**. Above 55%, long-video performance dropped ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/do-youtube-shorts-help-your-long-form-videos-grow-data-from-18000-channels)). Metricool found that channels posting about five Shorts a month got about twice the views per long video of channels with no Shorts ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/08/03/youtube-shorts-long-form-study-metricool/)). Both studies show a link, not proof of cause. One more fact changes how to read subscriber counts. YouTube's Todd Beaupré says subscribers skip about **90% of a channel's uploads**, and that subscribing "isn't as strong as a lot of people think" ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/subscribers-skip-90-of-uploads-in-their-feed-youtube-director-says/)). The right viewers matter more than the subscriber number.

YouTube's 2025–2026 rules shape how Chris should make Shorts. A Short can be up to 3 minutes long. But a Short over 1 minute with any Content ID claim is **blocked worldwide**, so any Short with licensed music should stay under 60 seconds ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en)). The Shorts feed first shows a new Short to a test group and judges it on "viewed vs swiped away" ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-march-4-2024/)). Galloway's data found that Shorts under 60% "viewed" did poorly, while 70–90% did well ([Digital Information World](https://www.digitalinformationworld.com/2023/04/paddy-galloway-decoded-youtube-shorts.html)). On length, the evidence is weak; his 2023 study found that 50–60 second Shorts drew the most views ([Adam Connell](https://adamconnell.me/youtube-shorts-statistics/)). Links in Shorts descriptions and comments are not clickable. **The only clickable path from a Short is the "related video" link**, which must point to a video on Chris's own channel ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14075157?hl=en)).

In July 2026 YouTube advised a spoken call to action in the last 5 seconds, such as "Get the full step-by-step breakdown by clicking the link right here". It also advised that the linked long video should deliver the Short's promise in its first 5–10 seconds, with no intro or logo ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-related-videos-traffic-guide/)). In September 2026 YouTube added Shorts series (seasons and episodes, for Partner Program creators) and made Posts recommendable in the Shorts feed ([Google Blog](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/connect-with-creators-on-the-biggest-screen-with-youtubes-latest-features/)). On 1 October 2026 it cut Shorts reach for channels that re-upload other creators' videos ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/10/02/youtube-wont-recommend-unoriginal-shorts/)). Chris films his own videos, so this change targets other channels, not him. Finally, YouTube's free Test & Compare tool now tests up to three titles and three thumbnails, judged on watch time, but only on long videos ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-title-a-b-testing-rolls-out-globally-to-creators/562571/)).

Instagram works differently. Instagram head Adam Mosseri names **watch time, likes and sends** (DM shares) as the top ranking signals, and says sends matter slightly more for reaching non-followers ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)). Reels up to 3 minutes can be shown to non-followers ([Instagram](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/the-latest-with-instagram)). Trial Reels show a Reel to non-followers first, so Chris can test hooks without filling his followers' feeds ([Instagram](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/instagram-trial-reels)). Since 30 April 2026, accounts that mostly post unoriginal content lose recommendations, and a watermark or caption credit does not make a re-upload original ([Instagram](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/rewarding-original-creators-on-instagram)). Clickable links live only in the bio, in Story link stickers, and in a paid test for Meta Verified users ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/social-media/meta-is-testing-clickable-links-in-instagram-captions-for-verified-subscribers-184555406.html)). Because TikTok is banned in India, Instagram and YouTube carry all short video for Chris's Indian viewers.

TikTok now runs in the US through a majority American-owned joint venture set up in January 2026 ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/announcement-from-the-new-tiktok-usds-joint-venture-llc?lang=en)). TikTok says follower count is not a direct ranking factor ([TikTok](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you?lang=en)). A Buffer study of 11.4M posts found that moving from one post a week to **two to five** raised views per post by 17%. More posts raised the chance of a breakout post, not the typical post's views ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)). Third-party guides say a bio link needs 1,000 followers or a Business account ([Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/blog/how-to-add-link-to-tiktok-bio)). TikTok is still banned in India, so the account should be run from the US side, not from India ([TTCalculator](https://ttcalculator.net/data/earnings/tiktok-pay-india-earnings/)). CapCut is also still blocked in India, so Chris should not build his editing around it ([jitendra.co](https://jitendra.co/capcut-in-india/)).

## Free calls, free events and email turned viewers into buyers

The channels that grew fastest sold the least. Rishabh Mishra has 594K subscribers but sells only 1:1 calls at ₹1,600–2,100 ([rishabhnmishra.com](https://www.rishabhnmishra.com/)). Akber Shaikh earns from sponsors and affiliate links ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@MediocreKuchKar/videos)). Ansh Lamba sells Udemy courses with a monthly coupon ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lkc-F5I8gFE)). Free full courses and broad roadmaps attract students and beginners. They rarely attract a working professional who is ready to pay for a premium program. So Chris needs two kinds of videos: **growth videos** that bring reach, and **qualifying videos** that speak only to a 2–7-year professional who wants to switch.

The closest model to Stackle is Sumit Mittal's TrendyTech. Its "Ultimate Data Engineering Masters Program" lists at ₹89,000, with an offer price of **₹79,000** (US$925–1,075 outside India) and six months of no-cost EMI ([trendytech.in](https://trendytech.in)). The site's main buttons are "Book Free Guidance Session", "Schedule call with learning counsellor" and "Request Whatsapp Chat", and the syllabus is sent on WhatsApp and email. TrendyTech claims 30,000+ learners and 400,000+ followers across LinkedIn and YouTube ([StreetInsider](https://www.streetinsider.com/Press+Releases/TrendyTech+Surpasses+30%2C000+Data+Engineering+Professionals+Trained/26860026.html)). Yet its YouTube channel reached 100K only after about five years. LinkedIn and the free call carried the business. GrowDataSkills runs a similar path at a lower price: a **₹13,000** bootcamp ([growdataskills.com](https://growdataskills.com/aws-data-engineering-live)), with "Limited Seats Only" and a call/WhatsApp number in its video descriptions ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@shashank_mishra/videos)).

The same conversion tools repeat across creators. The product link sits in the first lines of every description. A free lead magnet collects emails. Ashish Pratap Singh gives a free "System Design Interview Handbook" to newsletter subscribers ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@ashishps_1/videos)), and his newsletter reached about **60,000 readers in 10 months** ([AlgoMaster blog](https://blog.algomaster.io/p/introducing-weekly-premium-deep-dives)). Nate Herk promises a "Free Template" in his titles and sends viewers to a free Skool group, then sells a paid tier at about $99–129 a month ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9FuNtfsnRNo); [Skool](https://skool.com/ai-automation-society-plus/about)). Milestone videos become sales events. CampusX's "100K Special" and "200K Special" videos carried course discounts, and the 200K code expired after one week ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d4qykFC5Rso)). Lore So What, an Amazon analytics lead, uses an application instead of a sale: "I'll mentor you until you land a Data Job. 👉 Apply to my Academy." His channel earns the most subscribers per view of any channel we checked, about **45 per 1,000 views** ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@loresowhat)). Ali Abdaal rarely pitches his courses in free videos; he pitches them in email ([Schoolmaker](https://www.schoolmaker.com/breakdowns/ptya)). No creator publishes their viewer-to-call or call-to-sale rates, so Chris will have to measure his own.

The best evidence for moving a big audience onto YouTube is an event. Zach Wilson built his LinkedIn following first, posting daily: about 2,000 followers in December 2020, about 10,000 six months later, then **100,000 more in the next six months** ([Dutch Engineer](https://dutchengineer.substack.com/p/ask-a-data-mentor-zach-wilson-founder)). In November 2024 he announced "a free data engineering boot camp on YouTube on 11/15! It will be a new data engineering video every day from 11/15 until 12/31!" and asked people to share it ([X](https://x.com/Zachly/status/1855409271254725056)). The launch video has **409K views**, his biggest long-form moment ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=myhe0LXpCeo)). To get credit, students had to watch on his own platform and submit homework there ([DataExpert blog](https://blog.dataexpert.io/p/how-to-join-the-free-6-week-data)). He repeated the event in 2025 and ran an AI data engineering edition in 2026 ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IxvLvToD8nk)). He also talks directly to his most engaged followers. He "crawled his own LinkedIn posts to find people who engaged often, then spoke with them about what they liked and disliked" ([Dutch Engineer](https://dutchengineer.substack.com/p/ask-a-data-mentor-zach-wilson-founder)).

A big LinkedIn following does not turn into YouTube subscribers by itself. Austin Belcak is "massive on LinkedIn" but has about **3,000** YouTube subscribers ([Ali Abdaal](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LLjpnubsOWc)). Aishwarya Srinivasan has 500K+ LinkedIn followers, yet her podcast-style YouTube interviews drew only **600–1,800 views** each. She broke out only after she switched to explainers and career videos ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/@aishwaryasrinivasan/videos)). LinkedIn's March 2026 feed update targets "comment to agree" bait, engagement pods, comment automation and "videos that don't match the text" ([LinkedIn News](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed)). One study found that posts with links lose 26.5% of reach on average. The loss falls mostly on company pages; personal profiles show almost none. The study also found that the "link in first comment" trick no longer comes free ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-link-penalty-study)). For Chris, LinkedIn works best in three ways: to test ideas (posts that do unusually well become videos), to announce events, and to share a YouTube link in the post body on launch days.

Hormozi also warns against chasing the wrong views. In a 90-day test of mostly entertaining content, his views rose two to three times, but ad revenue fell by half: "we're actually getting the wrong people" ([Hormozi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jmkq5RLjm0U)). His month with the highest revenue per view also brought the most opt-ins and applications. He says "the content is the targeting", and that he looked at two businesses doing **over $1M a year with fewer than 5,000 followers** ([Hormozi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dMZ-n2KSlxE)). For Stackle, the goal is booked calls from the right person, not the biggest subscriber number.

## Conclusion: the playbook for Chris

This research changes the goal. "100K in six months" has no verified example in this niche, and even the best channels took 16–20 months. But the number that matters for Stackle is smaller and closer: a few thousand of the right viewers, meaning analysts, testers and junior engineers with 2–7 years of experience, who book a call. TrendyTech shows that a premium data engineering program can sell through free calls while YouTube grows slowly. So Chris should plan for 100K in **18–24 months**, and judge every month by **calls booked per video**. Even at the best lifetime averages in the niche (7,000–12,000 new subscribers a month), six months gives about 40,000–70,000 subscribers, and most channels grew more slowly than that in their first months.

Chris also starts with three advantages that most winners did not have at the start. His hiring-side proof (200+ interviews) is this niche's version of "Ex-Google", and it matches his viewer's pain, rejection, exactly. His 200K LinkedIn audience is a launch engine for events, which is the one LinkedIn-to-YouTube lever with evidence behind it. And two lanes are still open: the data-engineer-to-AI-engineer move taught from the hiring side, and careers for US visa-holders. The real risk is stopping. Every winner here kept a fixed weekly slot for one to two years. Sundas Khalid put it simply: "If you're not excited about it, you'll likely give up after a month or two" ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/ive-grown-income-700-600k-155555331.html)).

### 11. What to avoid

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| Chasing broad, entertaining views | Hormozi's views rose 2–3x while revenue halved: "the wrong people" |
| Daily Shorts with no long video behind them | Shorts turn views into subscribers at about one-third the rate; very high Shorts shares were linked to weaker long videos |
| Re-uploading other people's clips, or files with another app's watermark | YouTube (October 2026) and Instagram (April 2026) both cut reach for unoriginal content |
| Shorts over 60 seconds with licensed music | Any Content ID claim blocks them worldwide |
| "Comment X for the link", engagement pods or auto-comment tools on LinkedIn | LinkedIn's March 2026 update targets these |
| Using CapCut or TikTok from India | Both are blocked or banned there |
| Podcast-style interviews with lesser-known guests as the main format | Aishwarya Srinivasan's drew only 600–1,800 views each |
| Paying for two clipping tools | vidIQ Max and Opus Clip overlap |
| Stopping after three months | Every winner in this research kept a weekly slot for one to two years |
